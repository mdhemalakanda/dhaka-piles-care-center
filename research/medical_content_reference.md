# Medical Content Reference — Colorectal / Piles Clinic Website

**Purpose:** Ground-truth, patient-education copy for the six core content areas of the clinic website. Written in website-ready English so a developer can adapt it directly into condition pages. All clinical claims are aligned with the sources cited at the end of each condition.

**How to use this file**
- Each condition follows the same 7-section structure: What it is → Common symptoms → Causes & risk factors → When to see a doctor → Treatment options → Prevention & self-care → Recovery expectations.
- Copy is deliberately hedged. Do not strengthen it: no "cure", "guaranteed", "100% success", "painless forever", "permanent solution", and no success percentages anywhere on the site. Treatment choice always depends on the doctor's assessment of severity and individual findings.
- Replace "GP / NHS 111 / A&E" references from the UK sources with the clinic's own pathways (e.g., "see a doctor", "contact our clinic", "go to the nearest emergency department"). The emergency phrasings below are kept as red-flag guidance, not as UK-specific instructions.

**Responsible-language rules for the whole site (apply everywhere)**

## 1. Piles / Haemorrhoids (পাইলস / বোসা)

### What it is
Haemorrhoids, commonly called piles, are swollen veins in the lowest part of the rectum and around the anus — similar to varicose veins. They may form inside the anal canal (internal piles), where they are usually painless but can bleed, or under the skin around the anus (external piles), where they can itch, ache, or form a tender lump. Piles are very common, and many people experience them at some point. They are not dangerous in themselves, but the symptoms can overlap with more serious conditions, so new or persistent symptoms should always be assessed by a doctor.

### Common symptoms
- Bright red blood after passing stool — on the toilet paper, on the stool surface, or in the toilet bowl (often painless)
- An itchy or irritating feeling around the anus
- Pain or discomfort around the anus
- A lump around the anus, which may be tender
- Mucus in the underwear or on the toilet paper after wiping
- A feeling of still needing to pass stool after going to the toilet
- Internal piles can slip down (prolapse) through the anal opening, causing pain and irritation
- If a clot forms inside an external pile (a "thrombosed" pile): severe pain, swelling, inflammation, and a hard, discoloured lump near the anus

### Common causes & risk factors
Piles develop when the veins around the anus stretch under pressure. Things that increase the risk include:
- Constipation and straining on the toilet
- Sitting for long periods, especially on the toilet
- Long-lasting diarrhoea or constipation
- A low-fibre diet
- Being overweight
- Pregnancy (pressure from the growing baby)
- Regularly lifting heavy items
- Getting older (supporting tissues weaken and stretch)
- Frequent heavy lifting and, per Mayo Clinic, anal intercourse

### When to see a doctor
See a doctor for an examination rather than assuming bleeding is "just piles":
- Any bleeding from the back passage, especially the first time it happens — rectal bleeding can also come from other conditions, including colorectal cancer, so it needs proper evaluation
- Symptoms that do not improve after about a week of home care, or that keep coming back
- Any change in bowel habit, or a change in the colour or consistency of stool
- A noticeable change around the anus that is not normal for you

Seek urgent medical care if, alongside piles symptoms, there is:
- A very high temperature, or feeling hot, cold, shivery and generally unwell
- Pus leaking from the area
- Large amounts of bleeding, non-stop bleeding, large clots, severe pain, or light-headedness, dizziness or faintness — go to an emergency department immediately

### Treatment options
Treatment depends on the type and severity of the piles, and your doctor will recommend the option suited to you. Piles can come back even after successful treatment.

**Conservative / home care (often the first step):**
- Keep stools soft with plenty of fibre and fluids
- Warm baths (including sitz baths) to ease itching and pain
- An ice pack wrapped in a cloth to ease discomfort
- Paracetamol for pain; avoid painkillers containing codeine (they can cause constipation), and avoid ibuprofen if the piles are bleeding
- Keep the area clean and dry — pat rather than rub
- Avoid straining, and do not ignore the urge to pass stool

**Medicines (generic classes; a pharmacist or doctor can advise):**
- Topical creams, ointments or suppositories to ease pain, itching and swelling
- Laxatives or stool-softening treatment if constipation is contributing
- A pharmacist can suggest suitable products; a doctor may prescribe stronger treatment if needed

**Office / clinic procedures (usually done without an overnight stay):**
- Rubber band ligation — a small band is placed around the pile so it drops off
- Sclerotherapy — a solution is injected to shrink the pile
- Infrared coagulation or electrotherapy — energy is used to cut the blood supply so the pile shrinks

**Surgery (usually for large or persistent piles):**
- Haemorrhoidectomy — surgical removal of the piles
- Stapled haemorrhoidopexy — piles are stapled back inside the anal canal
- Haemorrhoidal artery ligation — stitches are used to reduce the blood supply so the piles shrink

### Prevention & self-care
- Eat more fibre gradually: fruits, vegetables, whole grains; fibre supplements such as psyllium can help, but drink plenty of water with them
- Drink enough fluids through the day; cutting down alcohol and caffeine may help
- Do not strain, and do not hold your breath on the toilet
- Go as soon as you feel the urge, and do not sit on the toilet longer than needed
- Stay active; exercise helps prevent constipation and reduces pressure on the veins
- Avoid long periods of sitting, and take breaks to move around

### Recovery expectations
Many people find their symptoms settle with home measures within about a week, though this varies. Office procedures are usually done with the area numbed and patients generally go home the same day. Surgery may need a short stay or same-day discharge depending on the person and the procedure. Recovery comfort and time differ from person to person, and piles can return later — your doctor will advise on follow-up and prevention.

## 2. Anal Fissure (ফিসার / মলদ্বারে চিড়া)

### What it is
An anal fissure is a small tear in the lining of the anal canal — the short passage at the end of the bowel. Because the area is rich in nerve endings and the surrounding muscle tends to tighten in response, a fissure typically causes sharp pain during and after passing stool. Fissures are common and often heal with simple self-care; ones that do not heal within a few weeks are called chronic fissures and usually need medical treatment. A long-standing fissure sometimes leaves a small skin tag near the tear, and it can occasionally be linked to other conditions, so persistent symptoms deserve a proper examination.

### Common symptoms
- Severe, sharp or burning pain when passing stool, which can continue for hours afterwards
- Bright red blood on the toilet paper, on the stool surface, or in the toilet water
- A visible small tear or crack at the edge of the anus
- Itching and irritation around the anus (more common in long-standing fissures)
- A small skin tag at the end of a chronic fissure
- Some people begin to avoid going to the toilet because of the pain, which can make constipation — and the fissure — worse

### Common causes & risk factors
- Constipation and passing large or hard stools (the most common trigger)
- Repeated or long-lasting diarrhoea
- Reduced blood flow to the anal sphincter muscle, which tightens and limits healing
- Pregnancy and vaginal childbirth
- Inflammatory bowel disease such as Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis
- Certain sexually transmitted infections
- Fissures are more common between the ages of 15 and 40, and in young children

### When to see a doctor
- See a doctor if symptoms are not improving after about a week of self-care, or if they are getting worse
- Any blood in the stool, in the toilet, or on the toilet paper should be assessed — bleeding can have several causes and should not simply be assumed to be a fissure
- Severe pain that makes you afraid to pass stool, or pain with fever, needs prompt assessment
- If a doctor is unsure of the diagnosis or suspects something more serious, they may arrange a specialist examination

### Treatment options
The right treatment depends on how long the fissure has been present and how severe it is; your doctor will advise.

**Conservative / home care:**
- Keep stools soft and easy to pass: plenty of fibre and fluids
- Warm, shallow baths (sitz baths) after passing stool to ease pain
- Paracetamol or ibuprofen for pain if suitable for you
- Keep the area clean and dry; avoid straining and do not ignore the urge to go

**Medicines (generic classes; prescribed as appropriate):**
- Laxatives if constipation is present
- Creams or ointments that help the tear heal — these may need to be used for several weeks (up to around 8 weeks per NHS guidance)
- Ointments that relax the anal muscle and improve local blood flow — prescription nitroglycerin (glyceryl trinitrate) ointment, or calcium-channel blocker ointments such as diltiazem or nifedipine
- Medicines to reduce pain after passing stool, as advised by a doctor

**Office / clinic procedures:**
- An injection of botulinum toxin (Botox) into the anal muscle to relax spasm so the tear can heal — a short procedure usually done under anaesthesia

**Surgery:**
- Lateral internal sphincterotomy — a small cut is made in the internal anal muscle to reduce tension and improve blood supply, allowing the tear to heal. This is considered when other treatments have not worked or fissures keep coming back. As with any surgery, benefits and risks (including a small risk of affecting bowel control) should be discussed with the surgeon.

### Prevention & self-care
- Prevent constipation: fibre-rich diet, plenty of fluids, and add fibre gradually
- Do not delay going to the toilet, and do not strain
- Use a warm bath after bowel movements while the fissure heals
- Avoid harsh wiping; keep the area clean and dry

### Recovery expectations
Most acute fissures heal with simple self-care within a few weeks, although healing times vary. Chronic fissures often need prescribed ointments, Botox treatment, or surgery, and healing after treatment can still take several weeks. Fissures can come back, especially if constipation continues, so keeping stools soft is the key long-term habit. Your doctor will advise on follow-up if symptoms persist.

**Sources:**
- NHS — Anal fissure: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/anal-fissure/
- WebMD — Anal fissure: https://www.webmd.com/digestive-disorders/anal-fissure
- Cleveland Clinic — Anal fissures (retrieved via search summary; direct page was not fetchable during research): https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/16913-anal-fissures

## 3. Anal Fistula (ফিস্টুলা / ভগন্দর)

### What it is
An anal fistula is a small tunnel that forms between the inside of the anal canal and the skin near the anus. It most often develops after an infection near the anus that creates a collection of pus (an abscess). When the pus drains away, it can leave a channel behind. A fistula usually does not heal on its own; symptoms tend to keep returning, sometimes in a cycle of temporary closure followed by renewed infection. Because of this, surgery is recommended in most cases, and the sooner it is assessed, the simpler the treatment is likely to be.

### Common symptoms
- Constant, throbbing pain around the anus, often worse when sitting, moving, coughing or passing stool
- Skin irritation around the anus
- Discharge of pus, blood, or foul-smelling fluid near the anus; some people see a small hole in the skin near the anus
- Swelling and redness around the anus, with a high temperature, if an abscess is also present
- The opening may seal over temporarily, causing pain and swelling to build up before it drains again
- In some cases, difficulty controlling bowel movements

### Common causes & risk factors
- Most fistulas develop after a perianal abscess, when the abscess does not heal completely after draining
- Less common causes include Crohn's disease and other inflammatory bowel disease, diverticulitis, hidradenitis suppurativa, tuberculosis or HIV infection, previous surgery near the anus, pelvic radiation therapy, and anal injury
- Higher risk in men, in people who have had a perianal abscess before, in people with inflammatory bowel disease, and in people with weakened immune systems
- About half of people with an infected anal gland may go on to develop a fistula (Cleveland Clinic)

### When to see a doctor
- See a doctor for any persistent anal pain or discharge — do not assume it will settle by itself, and do not assume it is piles; piles are usually not very painful
- Urgent assessment is needed if there is fever, swelling and redness around the anus, or rapidly increasing pain — these suggest an abscess that needs draining
- If a general doctor suspects a fistula, they will usually refer to a colorectal specialist; examination, a scope (proctoscopy/anoscopy), and scans such as ultrasound or MRI may be used to map the tunnel and plan treatment

### Treatment options
The type of surgery depends on the fistula's pathway and how much of the sphincter muscle it involves; your surgeon will discuss the options and their risks.

**Conservative care (supporting, not curative on its own):**
- Warm sitz baths, pain relief such as paracetamol, and keeping the area clean while awaiting treatment
- Antibiotics only when a doctor directs them (for example, for an associated infection); antibiotics alone do not close a fistula

**Procedures / surgery (the mainstay):**
- Fistulotomy — the whole length of a simple fistula is laid open so it can heal into a flat scar; typically used when only a small amount of muscle is involved
- Seton — a surgical thread is looped through the fistula and left in place for weeks to keep it draining and allow healing, often before or instead of a further procedure; commonly used for fistulas involving more muscle
- Endorectal advancement flap — a flap of healthy tissue is used to cover the internal opening, avoiding cutting the sphincter muscle
- LIFT procedure (ligation of the intersphincteric fistula tract) — the tunnel passing between the sphincter muscles is closed, usually after a seton stage
- Complex fistulas may need treatment in stages, and fistulas linked to inflammatory bowel disease may also be managed with medicines under a specialist (for example, biologic therapy)

### Prevention & self-care
- There is no reliable way to prevent a fistula, but prompt treatment of anal abscesses reduces the chance of one forming
- If you have Crohn's disease or another inflammatory bowel condition, keep it under specialist care
- After surgery: keep stools soft with fibre and fluids (pain medicines can cause constipation, so fibre supplements with plenty of water are often advised), take warm sitz baths, and follow wound-care and dressing instructions from the care team

### Recovery expectations
Most fistula procedures are day cases, though some people need an overnight stay or further staged operations. Recovery commonly takes several weeks and depends on how complex the fistula is. The wound may need dressings and time to heal from the inside out. Fistulas can come back after surgery, and there is a small risk of temporary or lasting effects on bowel control, which your surgeon will discuss. Keep all follow-up appointments so healing can be checked.

**Sources:**
- NHS — Anal fistula: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/anal-fistula/
- Cleveland Clinic — Anal fistula: https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/14466-anal-fistula

## 4. Pilonidal Disease / Pilonidal Sinus (পাইলোনিডাল সাইনাস)

### What it is
A pilonidal sinus is a small hole or tunnel in the skin at the top of the buttocks, in the cleft between them. Many people have one without noticing it. The problem usually starts when loose hairs, pushed in by pressure and friction, cause irritation — the body reacts to the hair like a splinter. If the sinus becomes infected, a painful pus-filled abscess (a pilonidal cyst/abscess) can develop quickly, making it hard to sit comfortably. Some people have repeated infections over time, which is called recurrent pilonidal disease.

### Common symptoms
- Often none at all until infection develops — just a small dimple or pit in the skin
- With infection: pain and swelling at the top of the cleft, developing quickly, often over a few days
- A pus-filled abscess that makes sitting painful or difficult
- Pus or blood draining from the area, sometimes with a foul smell
- Reddened, sore skin around the area; sometimes hair protruding from the lesion
- A low-grade fever in some people
- With recurrent disease: ongoing discomfort and repeated episodes of seepage or infection; sometimes more than one sinus tract (multiple openings)

### Common causes & risk factors
- The exact cause is not fully understood, but hair being forced under the skin by pressure or friction is thought to be central
- More common in men (who tend to be hairier), in young adults after puberty, and in people who sit for long periods — including long-distance drivers
- Friction from clothing, prolonged sitting, and local hygiene may all contribute

### When to see a doctor
- Seek an urgent appointment if you have a painful, bleeding, or pus-leaking lump at the top of the buttock cleft — these are signs of infection that need treatment
- Go urgently if pain is severe, fever develops, or the area is rapidly getting worse
- A non-infected sinus does not need surgery, but should still be assessed so a watch-and-wait plan and hygiene advice can be given

### Treatment options
Treatment depends on symptoms, the size of the sinus, and whether it is a first infection or a recurring problem; your doctor will advise.

**Conservative care (uninfected sinus):**
- Watch and wait, with regular washing of the area, keeping it clean and dry
- Do not shave the area unless a doctor advises it

**Medicines:**
- Painkillers such as paracetamol and, if suitable, anti-inflammatory medicines (NSAIDs) for pain and swelling
- Antibiotics may be given for infection, but they relieve infection rather than closing the sinus tract itself
- If an early case has little inflammation, a doctor may prescribe a broad-spectrum antibiotic with follow-up and hair/hygiene care (Healthline)

**Procedures:**
- Incision and drainage — for a painful abscess, a small cut is made to release the pus, usually under local or general anaesthetic; often arranged the same day, with regular dressing changes and sometimes a course of antibiotics afterwards
- Phenol injection — a chemical treatment injected into the sinus to harden and close it (used in some places; may need repeating, and recurrence is more likely with this method)

**Surgery (mainly for recurrent or extensive disease):**
- Wide excision with the wound left open to heal naturally — the sinus and some surrounding skin are removed; this has the lowest risk of the sinus returning but takes longer to heal with regular dressings
- Excision with wound closure — the sinus is removed and the skin stitched closed; recovery is usually quicker, but there is a higher risk of wound infection
- Endoscopic ablation — a thin camera is used to clean out hair and infected tissue and seal the sinus with heat; no external cut is needed, and full healing typically takes around a month or so
- Plastic surgery reconstruction, or less invasive options such as fibrin glue, may be considered in selected cases

### Prevention & self-care
- Wash the area daily, ideally in the shower; rinse well and keep the cleft completely dry
- Avoid sitting for very long periods; take breaks to stand and move
- Wear loose, comfortable cotton clothing and underwear
- After surgery: keep the area clean, avoid heavy lifting and strenuous exercise for about a week, avoid cycling for around 6–8 weeks, and do not swim until the wound has fully healed; eat enough fibre so passing stool is easy

### Recovery expectations
A drained abscess usually settles over a few weeks, with the wound healing from the inside out; overall treatment for an infected pilonidal sinus commonly takes several weeks depending on severity and method (Healthline describes roughly 4 to 10 weeks). Many people can return to desk-based work within about two weeks of surgery, though this varies with the procedure and the type of work. Excision wounds need regular dressing changes and patience, and a follow-up visit around six weeks is typical. The condition can recur even after surgery — especially the closed-wound approach — so hygiene habits and follow-up matter.

**Sources:**
- NHS — Pilonidal sinus: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/pilonidal-sinus/
- Healthline — Pilonidal sinus (pilonidal cyst): https://www.healthline.com/health/pilonidal-cyst

## 5. Anal Skin Tags & Related Anorectal Lumps (keeping it responsible)

### What it is
Anal skin tags are small, soft, benign flaps of excess skin around the anus. They are harmless in themselves and often remain after a swollen vein (a haemorrhoid) settles, or after an anal fissure heals — the swelling goes down but the stretched skin stays. Tags can also form from repeated friction or irritation. They usually cause no symptoms, though they can itch or make cleaning difficult. The important point for patients: any lump around the anus should be checked by a doctor first, because haemorrhoids, warts, a healing fissure, a clot, or — rarely — other conditions can look similar, and each is managed differently.

### Common symptoms
- Usually none; tags are typically painless and do not bleed unless irritated
- Mild itching, discomfort, or difficulty keeping the area clean after passing stool
- Skin-coloured soft lumps at the anal edge; they may grow slowly over time
- By contrast: haemorrhoids are often red or purple, can be painful and bleed on contact; warts are often rough-surfaced, may itch or burn, can grow larger and spread

### Common causes & risk factors
- A healed haemorrhoid — excess skin remains after the swelling subsides
- A healed anal fissure (tags linked to an untreated fissure tend to persist until the fissure itself is treated)
- Repeated friction or trauma — vigorous wiping, irritation, prolonged diarrhoea or constipation with straining
- Pregnancy, heavy lifting, blood clots in previous haemorrhoids
- Inflammatory conditions, especially Crohn's disease, can be associated with anal skin tags

### When to see a doctor
- See a doctor whenever a new lump appears around the anus, or if you are not sure whether it is a tag, a haemorrhoid, a wart, or something else — a simple examination usually sorts this out
- Seek assessment promptly if a lump is growing quickly, painful, bleeding, itchy or burning, changing in colour or appearance, or appears suddenly
- Seek urgent care for substantial or ongoing rectal bleeding, severe or increasing pain, fever, or pus/drainage
- Note on polyps: bowel polyps are growths inside the colon or rectum — they are different from external skin tags and are not visible from outside. Because polyps and other internal causes of bleeding are detected by endoscopy (colonoscopy), any bleeding from the back passage should be properly evaluated rather than assumed to be from an external lump.

### Treatment options
Most skin tags need no treatment at all. Removal is only considered for tags that cause persistent discomfort, itching, or hygiene problems — and it is a doctor-performed procedure, never a home remedy.

**Conservative care:**
- Gentle hygiene: clean and pat dry after bowel movements; avoid aggressive wiping
- Keep stools soft (fibre, fluids) to reduce friction and irritation
- Treat any underlying problem first — an active fissure or recurring piles — because removing a tag while the underlying condition continues may allow it to regrow

**Doctor-performed removal (small office procedure, when genuinely needed):**
- Surgical excision under local anaesthetic; laser removal; or cryotherapy (freezing with liquid nitrogen), depending on the tag and the doctor's judgement
- Some doctors remove only limited areas at a time to allow healing and reduce infection risk; a doctor may also advise leaving certain tags alone

**Important safety note:** Over-the-counter wart or skin-tag removers must not be used on anal skin. This skin is thin and sensitive, and self-treatment can cause burns, pain, and infection. Do not cut, tie off, or remove a tag yourself.

### Prevention & self-care
- Prevent constipation-related straining: adequate fibre and fluids
- Lubricating the area (for example, with petroleum jelly) before bowel movements can reduce friction if the skin is irritated
- Clean gently after each bowel movement and keep the area dry

### Recovery expectations
When a tag is removed in a small procedure, many people go home the same day, return to work the next day, and resume normal activities within about a week, though this varies (Healthline). Mild soreness is common for a few days; doctors often advise soft stools (laxatives or fibre), gentle hygiene, and may prescribe protective ointments or a short course of antibiotics. Infection can delay healing, so follow the wound-care instructions given. Tags can come back if the underlying cause — such as straining, friction, or an untreated fissure — continues.

**Sources:**
- Healthline — Anal skin tags: causes, removal, recovery: https://www.healthline.com/health/skin-disorders/anal-skin-tag
- Corroborated via search summaries of clinician resources (Cary Gastroenterology; colorectal specialist pages) on causes, "when to get checked", and doctor-only removal — see note in Appendix.

## 6. Constipation (কোষ্ঠকাঠিন্য) — the common driver

### What it is
Constipation means passing stool less often than usual, or passing stool that is hard, dry, or lumpy and difficult or painful to push out. A common benchmark is fewer than three bowel movements a week, or a feeling that the bowel has not emptied completely. It is one of the most common digestive complaints and affects people of all ages. Constipation matters in anorectal health because straining and hard stools are leading drivers of piles, anal fissures, and skin tags — treating constipation is often part of treating those conditions successfully.

### Common symptoms
- Fewer than three bowel movements in a week, or noticeably fewer than usual
- Stool that is unusually large or small, dry, hard or lumpy
- Straining or pain when passing stool
- A feeling of incomplete emptying afterwards
- Bloating, stomach ache, or feeling sick
- In long-standing constipation: leakage of loose stool around a build-up of hard stool (faecal impaction), which needs medical treatment

### Common causes & risk factors
- Not eating enough fibre (fruits, vegetables, cereals, whole grains)
- Not drinking enough fluids
- Low activity levels; long periods sitting or lying down
- Regularly ignoring or delaying the urge to go
- Changes in diet or daily routine; travel
- Medicines that can cause constipation, including opioid (codeine-type) painkillers — speak to a doctor before stopping any prescribed medicine
- Stress, anxiety or depression
- Pregnancy and the period after childbirth; older age (more common over 65)
- Occasionally an underlying medical condition — for example diverticular disease or, rarely, bowel cancer

### When to see a doctor
See a doctor if:
- Constipation does not improve with diet and lifestyle changes, or keeps coming back
- There is blood in the stool or bleeding from the back passage
- There is unexplained weight loss, ongoing tiredness, regular bloating, or tummy pain
- Bowel habits change suddenly and noticeably
- A medicine may be the cause — the doctor can adjust treatment; do not stop prescribed medicines on your own

Seek urgent care for severe abdominal pain, vomiting with inability to pass stool or wind, or heavy bleeding.

### Treatment options
**Diet and lifestyle (the foundation, usually tried first):**
- Gradually increase fibre: fruits, vegetables, whole grains; fruits containing sorbitol (apples, apricots, grapes/raisins, raspberries, strawberries) can help; wheat bran, oats or linseed can be added
- Drink plenty of water and other fluids; limit alcohol
- Keep a regular toilet routine, allow enough time, and do not delay the urge
- Resting feet on a low stool so knees are above hips makes passing stool easier
- A daily walk or other regular activity helps

**Medicines (generic classes; a pharmacist or doctor can advise):**
- Laxatives of different classes — bulk-forming (fibre-based), osmotic (draw water into the bowel), stool softeners, and stimulant laxatives — most work within a few days and are intended for short-term use unless a doctor advises otherwise
- For faecal impaction, a doctor may prescribe stronger laxatives, a suppository, or a mini enema; impacted stool should only be removed by a healthcare professional — never attempt this yourself

**Treating the related anorectal problems:**
- Where constipation has caused or is worsening piles, fissures, or skin tags, the doctor will treat the bowel habit and the anorectal condition together

### Prevention & self-care
- Eat enough fibre daily and increase it slowly so the body adjusts
- Drink adequate fluids; fibre works best with water
- Move regularly; avoid long sedentary stretches
- Respond promptly to the urge to pass stool; use a comfortable, unhurried toilet position
- Review constipating medicines with a doctor if relevant

### Recovery expectations
Diet and lifestyle changes often make a difference within a few days, though for some people it takes a few weeks (NHS). Most laxatives act within about three days. Long-standing constipation may take longer to settle and deserves medical review rather than continuous self-medication. Because constipation frequently returns if habits slip, the fibre-fluid-activity routine is the long-term fix — and it is also the main way to protect against piles and fissures coming back.

**Sources:**
- NHS — Constipation: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/constipation/
- NIDDK (NIH) — Constipation: https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/constipation

## Appendix — Cross-cutting red-flag box (recommended for the site)

A single shared red-flag panel can appear on every condition page:

> **Seek medical care promptly if you have:**
> - Bleeding from the back passage — especially the first time, heavy, non-stop, or with large clots
> - Bleeding together with dizziness, light-headedness or fainting
> - Severe or rapidly worsening pain
> - A high temperature with shivering and feeling generally unwell
> - Pus leaking from the anal area
> - A lump that is growing quickly or changing
> - A lasting change in your bowel habit, or blood mixed in with stool
>
> These symptoms can have several causes. Only a doctor's examination can identify the cause and the right treatment.
